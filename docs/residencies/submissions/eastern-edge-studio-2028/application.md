## Artist Bio (approx. 150 words)

Adam Simms is a media artist from Ktaqmkuk (Newfoundland), based in Tiohtià:ke (Montreal). Using cameras, data, and microelectronics, he creates works that treat environment and cultural memory as interconnected material.

For over a decade his work has centred on Pinchard's Island, the outport his grandmother resettled from in the 1960s. Through his family's history, he asks which aspects of intangible culture are portable and which are rooted in place.

*Cloudberry*, *Cabin*, and *Driftwood* used solar cameras, real-time data, and sensors to challenge remote presence, collapse distance, and transmit a disappearing outport vernacular to unexpected locations.

His work has appeared in *Riddle Fence* and *Ciel Variable*, and been exhibited at the Leonard & Bina Ellen Art Gallery and Occurrence. He holds a BFA in Design Art and an MFA in Studio Arts from Concordia University, where he has also taught as an Artist-in-Residence.

## Artist Statement outlining an overview of your practice/interests (max. 500 words)

Pinchard's Island is a treeless outport off the northeast coast of Ktaqmkuk (Newfoundland), reachable only when the ocean permits. My grandmother was born and raised there. In the 1960s, residents uprooted their homes and floated them across the bay until the community was reduced to a few gravestones. Years later, my extended family began going back and built an off-grid cabin. During those returns, we made soup, flew kites, told stories, and cooked toutons over an open fire. My family are collaborators in the work that came out of those returns, not subjects, with authority over their own voice and representation.

I am from Newfoundland, and I have lived away for half my life. Belonging remains partial, both from the place and outside it. I am unsettled, and I am resettled. That distance is the material of my work, a longing to trace the island's past with my footprints until it becomes a memory on the soles of my feet.

That longing led me to build cellular-connected cameras, microelectronics, and sensors powered only by the sun. A tether between Newfoundland and me. *Cloudberry* was a camera fixed to our cabin, photographing the same frame for over a year until there was not enough sunlight to run it. Each frame travelled by cellular network into a browser window, so my family could look out the cabin window from away. *Cabin* rebuilt a Newfoundland saltbox hearth in a Tiohtià:ke (Montreal) high-rise studio, with the live feed of Pinchard's Island as its window. *Driftwood* was a piece of wood from the Atlantic that rose and fell on live buoy data, moving with water a thousand kilometres away. Light House was a sculpture on the coastline that held a house in light, an afterlife for resettled homes. Together, the works treat presence as partial, remote, and permitted to fail. When they ended, nothing stayed behind.

The question underneath those works was how to represent a place that no longer exists. A more urgent one has since taken over. What is lost as the last generation of Pinchard's Islanders fades? My uncle's names for coves that are on no map, the way my grandmother bottles beets, the words I have heard nowhere else.

That urgency is turning me toward work meant to remain in place: a sculpture in the landscape that asks how much can be held, and what is lost, when an object outlasts its people and the knowledge they carry. Permanence and loss in the same field.

## Residency Proposal outlining your intended area of research/experimentation/creation (max. 500 words)

**THE SCULPTURE**

Glowberry is a six-foot bakeapple, 3D-printed from a photogrammetric scan of a single berry my grandmother picked on Pinchard's Island. It sits on the ground: too large, in the wrong place, clearly brought. The sculpture is built to weather the Ktaqmkuk (Newfoundland) landscape. You can walk around it, put a hand on it, lean against it. At night the light pulses through the berry, slow and amber, the way something breathes. It is meant to feel alive: still holding, still learning, still forgetting.

**WHY A BAKEAPPLE**

Bakeapple picking is one reason my family still returns to Pinchard's Island. A day bent over a bog fills a bucket. Resettlement took the houses but not the picking, which lives in whoever still knows which bog, when to go, how to make the jam, and who gets the jars. My grandmother is one of the last. Bakeapples are tiny, but what they carry is not, and the size of the sculpture nods to that disproportion. It is asked to hold everything Pinchard's knew.

**GETTING IT THERE**

The berry currently exists as a file: the photogrammetric scan is complete. I will 3D print the bakeapple in fall 2027 at Studio Artefact, a digital fabrication lab in Tiohtià:ke (Montreal), then drive it to St. John's myself in a U-Haul for the residency. Fabrication, freight, and siting are funded separately, through the Canada Council and CALQ. The permanent site for the sculpture is currently being evaluated, with permissions, insurance, and stewardship to be settled in 2027. The berry leaves Eastern Edge for its final location by the end of 2028. The residency fee covers my time, not the object.

**RESIDENCY PLAN**

I will arrive with the 3D-printed shell of the berry already UV and weather-coated (no painting, sanding, or dust on site). The remaining work is inside the berry: fitting the LEDs, spreading the light until it glows through the whole body, and programming a microcontroller to control the pulse. The question I am there to answer is what makes a light read as breathing. How slow, how deep, and whether it holds steady across a night can only be settled in front of the object. The light runs on a solar-charged battery. Once the pulse is set, I will be able to finalize its power requirements, so the battery and solar panel at the final site can be sized to support the sculpture through the seasons and their longest nights.

**OUTCOMES**

Towards the end of the residency, I will give an artist talk and light the berry for the public one evening. The lighting will happen in the Eastern Edge parking lot if permitted, otherwise the studio. Locals already know what a bakeapple is, and I want to witness their reaction to one this size. The outcomes of the residency are: a working lighting system, a photographic and written record of how people met the sculpture, and an absurd bakeapple ready for its final site.

## Summary outlining a brief, condensed version of your proposal (max. 50 words, 2 sentences)

Glowberry is a six-foot bakeapple, scanned from a single berry my grandmother picked on Pinchard's Island and asked to hold everything that island ever knew. At Eastern Edge I will teach it to breathe: a slow amber light, tuned in the dark, before it leaves for the coast.

## Cultural knowledge / languages (optional)

The word bakeapple is Newfoundland English; the same fruit is called cloudberry elsewhere, appik in Labrador Inuttitut, and aqpik in Inuktitut. The work keeps the Newfoundland word because the knowledge in it is local and comes from my own family, and from my grandmother in particular, who picked the berry the sculpture is scanned from. She has given her permission and is comfortable with how she is represented in the work. My relationship with her is lifelong, not one formed for this project.

I grew up with the word bakeapple and with the berry itself. The audience for this work is Newfoundlanders, as well as people living in and visiting Ktaqmkuk (Newfoundland). Resettlement is both a historical and a contemporary issue, and remains painful for many families. The work comes from one family's experience of resettlement, and does not claim to represent everyone's.

Bakeapples belong to more people than my family, and the sculpture does not speak for all of them. Anyone who knows the berry, whether from Newfoundland, Labrador, or further north, arrives with their own sense of what the berry is for, and I would rather the work make room for those meanings than stand in for them. I have no relationship to those communities' own traditions with the berry, and I am not claiming one. Scanning a single berry is what keeps that room open. A generic bakeapple would speak for everyone; this one speaks only for where it was picked.

## Technical and Other Supports

The sculpture is one piece, approximately 1.63 by 1.68 by 1.83 metres (64 by 66 by 72 inches), so it comes in through the garage door rather than the studio doors. It weighs an estimated 90 to 120 kilograms (200 to 265 pounds). I need about 4 by 4 metres (13 by 13 feet) of clear floor so I can walk around the sculpture, plus a work table, which fits within Studio 2's allotted space.

I will transport the sculpture myself with my own crew (local family and friends) and will arrange a ramp for the 0.3 metre (12-inch) step if needed; the same crew and route will handle load-out at the end of the residency.

The Glowberry lighting system runs on a battery I will provide, so power and wifi are needed for my laptop and to charge the battery. Because the work involves iterating with light, I am asking for access to the studio after dark. Nothing about this project is loud or requires ventilation.
